Module 1

Types & styles of wine

Every wine can be described by its type (still, sparkling or fortified), its colour, its sweetness and its body. Tap the cards to explore.

🍷

Still

No noticeable bubbles. The vast majority of wine.

  • Usually about 8–15% ABV.
  • Can be white, rosé or red; dry to sweet.
  • Examples: Chablis, Rioja, Sancerre, Barossa Shiraz.
🥂

Sparkling

Bubbles of carbon dioxide trapped in the wine.

  • CO₂ from a second fermentation in a sealed container is trapped, creating bubbles.
  • Usually around 12% ABV (some lighter).
  • Examples: Champagne, Cava (bottle) and Prosecco (tank).
🥃

Fortified

Grape spirit has been added to the wine.

  • Higher alcohol: usually 15–22% ABV.
  • Can be dry (e.g. Fino Sherry) or sweet (e.g. Port).
  • Examples: Port, Sherry.

Grape growing & winemaking

Wine is simply fermented grape juice — but the parts of the grape, the climate and the winemaker's choices shape everything in your glass.

The vine's year & ripening

☀️ Photosynthesis

Leaves use sunlight, carbon dioxide and water to make sugar, which is stored in the grapes. Vines also need warmth and nutrients.

🍇 Ripening

As grapes ripen: sugar rises, acidity falls, black grapes gain colour and tannins ripen. Fruit flavours move from green to riper.

✂️ Harvest

The grower picks when sugar, acid and flavour are in balance for the style wanted. Picked later = more sugar = more potential alcohol or sweetness.

Module 4

Tasting lab

The Level 1 Systematic Approach to Tasting follows three steps: look, smell, taste — then a conclusion. Build your own tasting note below.

👁️

Appearance

Tilt the glass against a white background. Note intensity (pale → deep) and colour: lemon/gold/amber; pink; purple/ruby/garnet/tawny. Whites deepen and reds pale (towards brown) with age.

👃

Nose

Swirl to release aromas, then sniff. Look for fruit (green, citrus, tropical, red, black), floral, spice, vegetal and oak notes (vanilla, toast).

👅

Palate

Sweetness (tip of tongue), acidity (mouth-watering), tannin (drying, grips gums), body (weight), flavours, finish (how long it lasts).

🧐

Conclusion

Is the wine balanced? Does it have flavour intensity and length? Do you like it — and why?

Module 5

Food & wine pairing

Food changes how a wine tastes. Pick a wine, then pick a food component and watch what happens to the wine on your palate. The dashed line shows the wine on its own.

Wine on its own

Sweetness HIGH RISK

Makes wine taste more bitter, more acidic and less fruity/sweet. Rule: the wine should be at least as sweet as the food.

Umami HIGH RISK

(Mushrooms, ripe cheese, cured meat, asparagus.) Like sweetness: wine seems more bitter, drying and acidic, less fruity. Salt helps balance it.

Acidity LOW RISK

Makes wine seem less acidic and richer, fruitier and sweeter. Pick a wine with at least as much acidity as the food.

Salt LOW RISK

Makes wine seem less bitter and less acidic, and smoother. Very wine-friendly.

Chilli heat HIGH RISK

Increases the burning sensation of alcohol, and bitterness. Choose lower-alcohol, fruity, off-dry whites.

Bitterness HIGH RISK

Bitter food increases bitterness in wine. Lower-tannin wines are safer.

Storage & serving essentials

🗄️ Storing

  • Store bottles with corks lying on their side so the cork stays moist and doesn't let air in.
  • Keep cool and at a constant temperature, in the dark, away from vibration and strong smells.

🍾 Opening sparkling

  • Chill well (reduces pressure).
  • Remove foil, loosen cage, keep a hand on the cork.
  • Tilt the bottle at ~45°, pointed away from people, and turn the bottle, not the cork. Ease it out with a gentle sigh, not a pop.

🍷 Glasses

  • Clean, odourless glasses (detergent and dust affect aroma).
  • Red: larger bowl. White/rosé: medium. Sparkling: flute. Fortified: small glass.
  • Fill only partly so the wine can be swirled.

💾 Leftovers

  • Re-seal and refrigerate.
  • Vacuum pump or inert gas slows oxidation of still wine.
  • Use a special sparkling-wine stopper for fizz.

⚠️ Faults

  • Cork taint: musty, damp cardboard smell; fruit dulled.
  • Oxidation: brown colour, flat, stale/nutty when it shouldn't be — e.g. a bottle left open too long.

🚦 Responsible drinking

  • Alcohol affects judgement and health. Never drink and drive.
  • At tastings, spit and drink water.
  • Know the guidelines where you live (e.g. UK: no more than 14 units a week, spread over 3+ days).

Units calculator

In the UK, 1 unit = 10 ml of pure alcohol. Units = ABV% × volume (litres).
